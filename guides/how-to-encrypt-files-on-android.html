How to encrypt files on your Android phone

Short answer: Every modern Android phone encrypts its storage, but that only protects your data while the phone is locked or switched off. Once you unlock it, every app with storage access, and anyone holding the phone, can read your files. File-level encryption keeps sensitive files locked even when the phone is open.

What Android's built-in encryption does

Since Android 10, phones use file-based encryption tied to your screen lock. If someone steals a locked phone, they can't read the storage. That's great, but it stops helping the moment the phone is unlocked.

When you need more

  • You lend your phone to someone or hand it over at a border.
  • You keep scans of your ID, passport, tax documents or medical records on it.
  • You don't want cloud backups to hold readable copies.

Method 1: Encrypted ZIP (7-Zip / ZArchiver)

Make an archive with AES-256 and a strong password. It's free and works anywhere, but it's awkward: you have to extract files to view them, and that leaves plaintext copies behind.

Method 2: An encrypted vault app

A vault encrypts each file when you import it and decrypts it only in memory while you look at it. Look for AES-256-GCM, a key derived from your PIN with a slow, memory-hard KDF such as Argon2id, and no server holding your key.

How Vireault's encryption works

When you create a vault, the app generates a random 256-bit key. That key is locked with a second key, which is derived from your PIN, pattern or passphrase using Argon2id (64 MiB of memory on most phones, 32 MiB on phones that can't spare it). Argon2id is deliberately expensive to run, which makes large-scale guessing slow. It can't make a short PIN strong, so new vaults need at least 6 digits, and a long passphrase is much stronger. New files are split into small chunks, each encrypted with AES-256-GCM and tied to that file, which also detects tampering. Neither key is stored in readable form anywhere, and nothing is sent to a server.
